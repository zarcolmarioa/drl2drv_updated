Hypothesis

This study employs novel active maximisation (AM) techniques to visualise different target layers of a Deep Convolutional Neural Network, the output of which has been constrained by a pre-trained deep generator network. We theorise that the images generated by this methodology closely imitate the phenomenology of visual hallucinations (VH). The hallucination categories simulated by targeting different layers of the model and applying different AM techniques include simple or complex hallucinations, caused by neurological (Parkinson’s disease, Lewy Body dementia or eye disease) or psychedelic conditions (ingestion of hallucinogens). Simple VHs involve flashes, lines, abstract-coloured shapes, and repeating patterns, while complex VHs involve objects, people, animals, buildings and landscapes. We hypothesise that people who have experienced psychedelic visual hallucinations will report a high degree of similarity between their recalled experience and the simulations of simple and complex psychedelic hallucinations generated by our model. We will test this main hypothesis using an experiment, which asks participants who have had psychedelic VHs within the last year to choose the image which best resembles their recalled experience, from a selection of model-generated simulations of neurological and psychedelic hallucinations. We shall present stimuli in four counterbalanced blocks, with two of these blocks only using simple visualisations and the other two only using complex visualisations. We predict that, on average, participants will select the simple and complex variants of psychedelic VH simulations more frequently than they select simple and complex variants of the neurological VH simulations. This would provide evidence to validate the model used. 
Dependent variable

The key dependant variable is the perceived phenomenological similarity between real life visual hallucinations induced by psychedelic substances, and simulations of psychedelic visual hallucinations generated by the computational model.  This variable is indexed by the frequency that participants select simulations of psychedelic hallucinations generated by our model, rather than simulations of neurologic hallucinations. A single data point reflecting the percentage of psychedelic images selected shall be calculated for each participant, by summing the times they selected psychedelic stimuli across trials and dividing this total by the number of trials to give the mean value of psychedelic image selections. The values reflecting each participant’s average selection of psychedelic images across trials shall be summed and divided by the number of participants (200) to give the sample mean of psychedelic simulation selection frequency.  This process shall be applied to the data of both part one and part two of the experiment, giving the total frequencies of psychedelic image selection for simple and complex stimuli, respectively. 
Conditions

The model has four parameters: Hallucination Type (Neurological vs Psychedelic), Iterations (1, 2 or 3 levels), Target Layer (conv3, conv4, fc8) and AM Type (Fixed vs Winner-Takes-All). However, only the AM technique of ‘winner-takes-all’ is used to generate the simple stimuli, and only target layer fc8 is used to generate the complex stimuli. Thus, there are three conditions with different levels in the experimental blocks presenting simple stimuli (Hallucination Type, Iterations and Target Layer) and complex stimuli (Hallucination Type, Iterations and AM Type). As these result from applying different manipulations to different properties of the computational model, they are all variations of the same independent variable of visual hallucination simulation. Stimuli from the three different conditions are simultaneously and randomly presented to each participant during the experimental task trials, in part one and two respectively.  
 
1.	Target layer: Images that simulate simple visual hallucinations have been generated by targeting either layer conv3 or layer conv4 in the deep convolutional neural network, while visualisations of complex hallucinations have been created by targeting only layer fc8. Thus, blocks using the simple stimuli present two levels of target layers while this is not varied in the blocks using complex stimuli.
 
2.	Hallucination Type (2 levels): Images which simulate neurological VHs have been generated by combining a Deep Convolutional Neural Network with a pre-trained deep generator network. On the other hand, to simulate psychedelic VHs, the DGN is disabled and only the perceptual network is used to generate output images. Both simple and complex blocks present an equal number of psychedelic and neurological stimuli.
 
3.	Iterations (3 levels): Iterations, via backpropagation, calculate how the colour of each pixel in the image should be altered to increase the activation of the target neuron during AM. This experiment uses stimuli produced by three different levels of iterations which are equally represented in individual trials. 
 
4.	AM Type: Active Maximisation is a visualisation technique in which an image is generated that maximises activity of either a target neuron or an entire layer of the network. Our model implements two AM techniques: ‘winner-takes-all’ and ‘fixed’. In the former, instead of activating a predetermined single target neuron, or multiple neurons, we allow all neurons in the network to be considered and select the target neuron based on the strongest response to the input image. In the latter, the target neuron to be activated is fixed.  The simple blocks only use winner-takes-all AM, while the complex blocks use both techniques.  
Analyses

1.	To investigate whether the simulations of simple and complex psychedelic hallucinations generated by our model are phenomenologically similar to psychedelic experiences induced by hallucinogens, we shall calculate whether participant selection frequency of simple and complex psychedelic stimuli is significantly statistically higher than chance level. A one-tailed one sample t-test using the test variable of sample mean and test value of a chance level of 0.5 (50%) shall be compared, for the both the simple and complex stimuli blocks. A resulting p value equal to or less than 0.05 (P ≤ 0.05) will be interpreted as evidence supporting the alternative hypothesis that participants select psychedelic VH simulations at a higher than chance frequency (and neurological VH simulations below chance frequency), as they are phenomenologically similar to psychedelic experiences induced by hallucinogens. This simple analysis shall be followed by a Bayesian t-test.

2.	We shall conduct a secondary set of analyses for the simple and complex blocks, to investigate how the various manipulations applied to different properties of the computational model affect perception of phenomenologically similarity between outcome stimuli and real-life psychedelic experiences. Presuming our hypothesis is correct, the results of the previous analysis will reveal that participant average selection of psychedelic VH simulations is higher than chance level, and correspondingly, selection of neurological VH simulations is below chance level. This would provide evidence that the psychedelic VHs generated by our model are phenomenologically similar to psychedelic experiences induced by hallucinogens, while the neurological VH simulations are significantly phenomenologically different. In this instance, we will only be interested in how different parameter levels affect stimuli selection within the psychedelic bracket. For the simple blocks, we will examine how iteration level and target layer affects psychedelic stimuli selection, as AM type is fastened as winner-takes-all. For the complex blocks, we will examine how iteration level and AM type affects stimuli selection, as target layer is fastened at layer fc8. We shall follow each simple analysis with a Bayesian t-test. Conversely, if analysis 1 yields the unexpected result that the visualisations of neurological hallucinations are selected significantly more than chance level, we shall run the following analysis with the neurological instead of psychedelic stimuli selection data. 
 
a.	Target layer: A single data point for each participant will be calculated which reflects the frequency that they selected psychedelic stimuli generated by targeting layer conv3, by summing the frequency that they selected psychedelic conv3 stimuli across trials and dividing this total by the number of trials where they selected psychedelic stimuli. This provides the mean percentage that each participant selected psychedelic simulations, generated by targeting layer conv3. Participant means will be summed and divided by the number of participants (200) to give the sample mean percentage of psychedelic conv3 layer selection frequency.  A two-tailed one sample t-test using the test variable of sample mean and test value of a chance level of 0.5 (50%) will be run to establish whether participants selected stimuli generated by targeting layer conv3 or conv4 significantly more than chance level. The results of this analysis will imply whether targeting a particular layer in the neural network produces visualisations which are of greater phenomenological similarity to real-life psychedelic experiences. 
 
b.	AM type: A single data point reflecting the frequency that each participant selected stimuli generated using the ‘winner-takes-all’ AM technique shall be calculated by summing the frequency that participants selected psychedelic ‘winner-takes-all’ stimuli across trials and dividing this total by the number of trials where they selected psychedelic stimuli. This provides the mean percentage of ‘winner-takes-all’ selections in the psychedelic category for each participant. Participant’s mean percentages shall be summed together and divided by the number of participants (200) to give the sample frequency of psychedelic ‘winner-takes-all’ generated stimuli selection.  A two-tailed One Sample t-test using the test variable of sample mean percentage and test value of a chance level of 0.5 (50%) will be run to establish whether participants selected stimuli generated via the ‘winner-takes-all’ AM technique at a significantly different level to chance. The results of this analysis will imply whether the AM technique used to generate stimuli affects the phenomenological similarity of output to real-life psychedelic experience. 

c.	Iteration Level: First, the frequency that each participant selected psychedelic stimuli generated by the three different levels shall be independently summed and divided by their total frequency of psychedelic stimuli selections across trials. Next, sample means for the frequency selection of psychedelic stimuli generated by application of the three iteration levels shall be calculated by summing each participant’s mean selection frequency for the respective levels and dividing the sums by the number of participants (200). This provides three data points reflecting the frequency that the sample selected psychedelic stimuli generated by the application of three different iteration levels. Three two-tailed one sample-t tests comparing the test value of chance level 0.5 (50%) against each iteration sample mean will be conducted, to establish whether the iteration level of the stimuli affects perception of phenomenological similarity to psychedelic hallucinations significantly different from chance level. Bonferroni corrections shall be applied to correct for multi-comparison.
Outliers and Exclusions

While we have instructed participants to choose the image which most resembles their psychedelic experience, it could occur that participants think none of the available images are remotely similar. In this instance, to avoid participants randomly selecting images and minimise noise, we have included a ‘skip’ button to be used if none of the images is thought to be appropriate. During analysis, we will exclude the data of participants who skip more than 75% of the whole trial, because it will be assumed that these cases too advantage of the ‘Skip’ option to speed through the experiment without complying.
It is possible that a minority of participants will not pay full attention to the image selection task due to tiredness, boredom or distraction. It is also possible that they use the participatory link multiple times and select images randomly, as their sole motivation for participating is to be entered into the prize draw rather than also providing accurate information. Due to the form of our experiment as an anonymous online survey conducted on participant’s personal computers, the experimenters cannot observe indications of such behaviour and offset it in person or rule out duplicate participations. Therefore, we shall exert several rules to exclude potential cases of this nature. Firstly, if participant average reaction time - as measured on Gorilla – is under 1 second, we shall assume that this participant has been clicking randomly and exclude their data. Secondly, a forced-choice format and response time limit of 1 minute per trial will be implemented to avoid distraction by removing the opportunity for wandering attention and long breaks. The next trial will automatically begin if an answer is not given within the time limit, and any participants who do not meet the time limit on at least 50% of trials shall be assumed to not be paying attention or allowing the experiment to run without providing an answer. 
It is also possible that a computer or internet fault makes the collected data unusable. If there is a server error or the WIFI connection is unstable, the stimuli might not load correctly. Participants have been asked to report any unloaded images by pressing the space bar, before selecting the best image available. If - after data collection - we find cases where the stimuli did not load correctly in more than 5% of trials, we shall exclude these cases from analysis. 
Any outliers in data following the application of these instances shall be treated as naturally occurring variance in participant experience. 
Sample Size

Despite conducting this experiment to test a main hypothesis, there are other non-directional sub questions we are hoping to gain insight to with the data collected, as explained in the ‘other’ section. Thus, we have decided it is suitable to run the experiment as a survey and have analysed the required sample size of 200 participants by drawing upon Home Office national statistics on drug misuse, data from The Psychedelic Society, UK, and previous relevant publications. 
 
The 2018/19 Crime Survey for England and Wales (CSEW) found that in a nationally representative sample of residents aged 16 to 59 in households in England and Wales (approximately 35 million), 0.4% reported using LSD (approximately 140 thousand) and 0.5% reported using Magic Mushrooms (approximately 175 thousand). Using a confidence level of 95% and error margin of 7%, both these population sizes project a sample size of 196 participants. This report can be found at: https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/832533/drug-misuse-2019-hosb2119.pdf. Limitations of the CSEW are that it excludes Scotland and Northern Ireland, as well as psychedelic drug users over 59 and those who are not part of the household resident population covered by the survey. Thus, we have also considered information obtained from The Psychedelic Society, UK, which reports that an expected 25% (17,500) of the 70,000 people signed up to their mailing list are interested in LSD, DMT and psilocybin. Using a confidence level of 95% and error margin of 7%, this population estimate projects a sample size of 194 participants. Upon the consideration that there are many psychedelic drug users in the general population who do not belong to a psychedelic society or are unwilling to participate in national surveys, we have compared this sample size to previous relevant publications. To the extent of our knowledge, our proposed experiment is the first survey-style study of VH phenomenology which utilises stimuli generated by visualising the layers of a deep convolutional neural network combined with a pre-trained deep generator network through active maximisation. However, previous survey studies have used other mediums to compare relevant stimuli with first-hand subjective reports of psychedelic experiences. One such study by Dubois and VanRullen (2011) asked LSD users to decide which of ten snapshot movies simulating visual trails best matched their recollected experience, finding that more than 210 participants consistently preferred faster snapshot intervals. This sample size aligns well with the sample sizes sourced from CSEW and The Psychedelic Society data, and the mean of these three sample sizes gives us the final recruitment number of 200 participants. 
Other

1.	Correlation between potency and iteration: We are interested in whether variations in the phenomenological characteristics of simulated VHs - caused by the application of different iteration levels - corelates with the potency of the psychedelic experience upon which participants base their stimuli selections. For each participant we will run a simple linear regression plotting iteration level against frequency of stimuli selection for both simple and complex simulations. The gradient value shall be used as the single data point reflecting the frequency of iteration level selected by each participant. We will then run a one-tailed bivariate correlation (Pearson’s coefficient) using the variables of participant iteration selection (gradient) and psychedelic experience potency rating, to calculate the correlation coefficient which evaluates the strength of the relationship between reported potency of psychedelic experience and iteration level of the stimuli selected. 
 
2.	Perceptual Veridicality: We are interested in the phenomenological similarity of psychedelic experiences induced by hallucinogens to everyday experiences when the perceiver has not ingested any hallucinogens, compared to the phenomenological similarity of neurological hallucinations induced by neurological conditions to normal visual experiences. Each participant shall be asked to rate the perceptual veridicality of the simple and complex aspects of their psychedelic experience on Likert scales from 1 (completely different) to 10 (identical) using the question ‘On a scale of 1 (completely different) to 10 (identical), how similar were the simple/complex aspects of your psychedelic experience, to the way things appear when you have not ingested any hallucinogens?’. The mean perceptual veridicality of the simple and complex aspects of participant’s psychedelic experiences will be calculated by dividing the sum of all participant’s ratings by the number of participants (200). An independent samples t-test followed by a Bayesian t-test will then compare these values to the mean perceptual veridicality of simple and complex neurological VHs, calculated from data obtained in a different experiment run by the research group. 

3.	Spontaneity:   We are interested in the spontenaity of psychedelic visual hallucinations compared to neurological visual hallucinations. By spontaneity, we mean whether the VHs experienced are dependent upon or entirely independent from existing visual stimuli. Each participant shall be asked ‘On the following scale from 1 (Completely Dependent) to 5 (Completely Independent), please indicate the extent to which your hallucinations were dependent upon or independent of existing visual content’. This will provide data reflecting the spontaneity of each participant’s psychedelic VHs, which will be averaged to find the mean spontaneity of all participant psychedelic VHs. An independent samples t-test followed by a Bayesian t-test will then compare this value to the mean spontaneity of neurological VHs, calculated from data obtained in a different experiment run by the research group.
